A few weeks ago, China's Ministry of Commerce (Mofcom) published [Announcement No. 48](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/), placing 'frontier' artificial intelligence technologies under an export control regime for the first time. We're talking about large language models, next-generation image recognition systems, and cutting-edge inference technology. Until now, it was mostly the US making export control headlines — restricting chips, software, and AI infrastructure from reaching China. Now China is responding with its own version of the same playbook.
If you're building or investing in a tech startup, this is not the kind of news you can comfortably scroll past. There are model supply chains, API licenses, and cloud dependencies that may already be affected. For an ecosystem like Indonesia's, which increasingly relies on foreign AI infrastructure, the question becomes sharper: dependent on whom?
This isn't a legal analysis. I'm a developer trying to understand the practical consequences — especially for people building or funding tech businesses here.
## What Mofcom No. 48 Actually Covers
The regulation puts frontier AI — a category covering large language models, advanced generative systems, and state-of-the-art inference technology — on a list requiring export licenses from the Chinese government. Chinese companies can no longer freely sell, transfer, or license this technology to foreign entities without Mofcom approval.
[Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/) that this move is seen as a mirror image of the export controls the US already applies to chips and AI software. China frames it as protecting national security interests and technological sovereignty — a phrase that's now standard boilerplate for any major economy with strategic digital assets.
The key detail: this is not an outright ban. It's a licensing system. But in practice, an opaque licensing system is a real barrier — especially for small and mid-sized companies that lack the resources to navigate two governments' bureaucracies simultaneously.
## Who Gets Hit First
Companies that already partner with Chinese AI vendors — through APIs, model licenses, or research collaborations — will feel the friction earliest. [TechCrunch has noted](https://www.techcrunch.com/) that several global AI startups relying on Chinese-built models or infrastructure have already started auditing their dependency chains.
In Indonesia, our startup ecosystem sits in an interesting spot. Many companies use a mix: OpenAI or Anthropic models for language tasks, open-source models partly originating from Chinese research labs (like Qwen from Alibaba or Yi from 01.AI), and sometimes cloud services also based in China. If any one layer gets caught in the regulatory net, the ripple effects can be significant.
For investors, this is also a due diligence question. <TypographyMark>Startups with deep dependency on a single vendor or a single AI ecosystem will look riskier</TypographyMark> to Series A and later-stage investors, particularly those with institutional LPs from Western markets.
## The Hidden Dependency Problem
This is what often gets skipped in everyday conversations. A lot of teams know that GPT-4 or Claude sits behind their product. But fewer trace where the open-source model they fine-tuned actually originated, or who controls the cloud infrastructure it runs on.
<TypographyMuted as='span'>Models like Qwen2.5 and DeepSeek-R1 were released as open-source and have been downloaded millions of times on Hugging Face. China's export controls don't automatically revoke access to already-distributed models — but they could affect future updates, commercial fine-tuning arrangements, and technical support.</TypographyMuted>
These hidden dependencies are a real engineering problem. Small technical decisions made early — picking a library or model because it was easy and free — can become core dependencies that are hard to swap out later. In a more geopolitically competitive environment, those early calls carry bigger business consequences.
## What This Means for Funding and Investment
If you're in a fundraising process or preparing your startup for the next stage, a few things are becoming standard investor concerns:
First, **AI vendor diversification**. Geopolitically aware investors will ask: what happens if one of your primary AI vendors becomes inaccessible? Having a plan B is no longer optional — it's a baseline expectation.
Second, **data residency and sovereignty**. Export controls on AI rarely travel alone — they often come alongside data regulations that follow similar logic. [Ars Technica has covered](https://arstechnica.com/) at length how AI and data policy in major economies ripples out into dependent markets.
Third, <TypographyMark>startups that can demonstrate a genuinely vendor-agnostic architecture</TypographyMark> — able to swap models or infrastructure without a full rebuild — will have stronger appeal to institutional investors. This isn't just a technical virtue; it's a business argument.
For the local ecosystem, this is also an interesting moment to ask whether there's room for locally-built AI models — not out of narrow technological nationalism, but because there's a genuine operational need for solutions that don't depend on regulatory decisions made in Beijing or Washington.
## How Indonesian Startups Should Read This
Honestly: this regulation won't directly affect most Indonesian startups tomorrow morning. But the pattern taking shape — where frontier AI becomes a state-controlled strategic asset — is worth internalizing now.
We're already used to operating between two large ecosystems. Many of our products run on American infrastructure (AWS, GCP, Azure) while also using components from the Chinese ecosystem (e-commerce tools, payments, and now AI). When geopolitics was relatively stable, this wasn't a problem. As both sides start putting up fences, our position in the middle could be an advantage — or a vulnerability.
The advantage: we're not deeply committed to either camp, so we have flexibility. The vulnerability: we have no real leverage in either camp, so when rules change, we can only react.
The most actionable step today is a dependency audit. Know exactly what's in your stack, where it comes from, and what the alternatives are. That's the most concrete thing any developer or founder can do right now — before a regulatory announcement from Beijing or Washington forces you to do it under pressure.
